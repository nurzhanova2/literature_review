# Literature Review

## Explainable Artificial Intelligence for Early Identification of Credit Risk and Latent Borrower Deterioration

---

## 1. Scope of the review and formulation of the problem
Credit-risk assessment estimates and ranks the likelihood that a borrower will fail to meet contractual obligations, using information that is necessarily incomplete when the assessment is made. For most of the field’s history, this task has been addressed with statistical scorecards and logistic regression. Their appeal lies in a relatively transparent mapping from borrower characteristics to a risk estimate, which also makes them a natural benchmark for more complex methods [1], [2]. Three later developments have reshaped the field: flexible machine-learning (ML) classifiers, post-hoc methods for explaining their predictions, and the growing use of temporal information about how borrower risk evolves. The present review examines where these three strands meet, and it focuses specifically on the early identification of borrower deterioration before formal default.

A preliminary distinction concerns the unit of analysis. The literature labelled “credit risk” or “financial risk warning” covers several related but non-equivalent prediction targets. These include individual-borrower and loan default [3], [4], [5], [6], corporate loan default [7], financial distress of listed companies [8], [9], [10], sector-level or industry-specific risk [11], [12], supply-chain finance risk [13], severe profitability deterioration [14], bank default [15], and systemic financial risk [16], [17]. The targets differ in the economic event predicted, observation frequency, available covariates, class prevalence, action horizon, and the costs of different errors. Methodological insights can transfer across these settings. Evidence of predictive success for listed-company distress or systemic risk, however, cannot be treated as direct evidence of effectiveness for borrower-level monitoring. The present study therefore takes as its unit of analysis borrower i observed at time t.

A second distinction concerns the outcome. In this review, formal default denotes an adverse credit event defined by an institution, a regulation, or a documented portfolio policy. Latent deterioration denotes an earlier, observable worsening in borrower quality that precedes formal default but is not identical to it. Early warning denotes a signal generated early enough to permit intervention, not merely the correct prediction of an eventual event. These definitions are the conceptual framing adopted here rather than terms standardised in the reviewed literature. They matter because a model can predict eventual default accurately and still provide no operationally useful advance signal. The problem that motivates this review is therefore broader than whether a borrower will default. The question is whether deterioration can be detected early enough, with reliable risk estimates, a tolerable false-alert burden, and explanations stable enough to support monitoring and audit.

## 2. From statistical scoring to machine-learning ensembles: evidence and its limits
The move from conventional scoring toward ML is usually motivated by the capacity of non-linear methods to represent interactions, heterogeneous borrower profiles, and non-additive relationships. Random Forest provided one of the foundational ensemble formulations [18], and gradient-boosting variants such as XGBoost and LightGBM now dominate applied comparisons [3], [5], [19], [20], [21]. Breiman’s contribution is methodological, however. It establishes the properties of an algorithm and does not constitute evidence that the algorithm is superior on credit portfolios.

The benchmarking tradition makes this distinction concrete. Baesens et al. compared a wide range of classifiers across eight real-life credit-scoring datasets. Several advanced methods performed well, but simpler techniques such as logistic regression and linear discriminant analysis remained competitive [1]. The methodological implication is that the value of added complexity is an empirical question. It has to be answered by comparing models under the same information set, target definition, sampling scheme, and validation protocol. Headline AUC or accuracy figures drawn from different studies cannot answer it.

More recent studies generally report advantages for ensemble or hybrid models in specific settings. These include P2P lending [3], credit-default prediction with gradient-boosting ensembles [5], [19], comparisons of Random Forest and XGBoost for loan default in a banking context [21], corporate loan default in the Slovak banking sector [7], and personal credit risk modelled with a hybrid CNN–Transformer architecture [6]. Taken together, these results show that non-linear models can add predictive value. They do not, however, establish a stable ranking of algorithms, because the studies differ in datasets, targets, class prevalence, borrower populations, and validation designs. Comparisons within a single study are informative. Comparisons across studies are largely not.

The collective evidence therefore supports a conditional trade-off rather than the replacement of statistical scoring by ML. Flexible models may capture structure that linear scorecards miss. Transparent baselines retain value for interpretation, benchmarking, calibration comparison, and governance [1], [2]. Several recent proposals try to narrow this trade-off directly instead of accepting it. Examples include building scorecards from SHAP values of a LightGBM model [20], constraining model complexity while allowing selected interactions for tabular data [22], and boosting with piecewise-polynomial components [23]. The attention these approaches receive suggests that the field increasingly treats performance and interpretability as quantities to be jointly optimised and not as mutually exclusive choices.

## 3. Explainability in credit-risk models
As models become more complex, interpretability and explainability separate into distinct methodological requirements. An intrinsically interpretable model, such as a scorecard or a suitably specified logistic regression, exposes its structure directly. A post-hoc explanation describes the behaviour of a model that has already been trained. Within post-hoc methods, global explanations describe feature influence across a population, while local explanations describe the contributions to a single prediction. LIME introduced a general local-surrogate framework for explaining individual predictions of any classifier [24]. SHAP later unified several additive attribution methods within a Shapley-value framework [25]. The two methods rest on different assumptions and answer different questions, so they are not interchangeable labels for “interpretability”. Neither establishes that a feature causally affects default; each describes the behaviour of a fitted model [24], [25]. Where causal claims are the goal, a separate line of work uses causal machine learning, for example Bayesian-network learning for default inference [26].

Post-hoc explanation, SHAP in particular, has spread rapidly across credit-risk applications. It has been used to explain granting models in P2P lending [3], risk models in fintech and SME credit [27], [28], credit-default ensembles [5], [19], and profit-sensitive classifiers for small businesses in P2P lending [29]. Beyond description, SHAP values are increasingly used as inputs to the modelling process itself: for scorecard construction [20], for stepwise feature selection [30], and for analysing network-augmented features and outlying borrowers [31], a line that connects to earlier network-based credit-risk models [32]. Explanation methods have also been extended to questions of fairness and bias, including structural causal models combined with XAI [33], differentially private SHAP-based frameworks [34], and analysis of proxy leakage of protected attributes [35]. Comparative assessments of explanation behaviour across architectures have likewise begun to appear [6].

Bücker et al. provide the most explicit conceptual framing of these developments. They treat transparency, auditability, and explainability as distinct requirements for ML credit scoring and argue that predictive performance alone does not meet the need for model understanding [2]. Their argument is directly relevant to financial institutions, where model outputs must be traced, reviewed, challenged, and documented.

A recurring limitation across the applied SHAP literature is that an explanation computed at one point in time tends to be read as a stable property of the borrower or of the underlying economic mechanism. This reading is an inference that the methods themselves do not license, and the point is a synthesis offered in this review rather than a claim stated in the cited sources. Attributions are conditional on the trained model, the background distribution, the dependence structure among features, and the composition of the data [24], [25]. For monitoring, then, the question is not only whether an explanation can be produced. It is also whether the explanation remains stable when the model is applied across periods and changing portfolio conditions.

## 4. Class imbalance and the stability of explanations
Default is usually a minority event, so class imbalance is both a predictive and an interpretive problem. Resampling, class weighting, and threshold choices change the fitted decision boundary, and they can therefore change the explanations attached to predictions as well as recall and precision. Chen, Calabrese and Martin-Barragan provide direct evidence on this point: in credit scoring, the stability of LIME and SHAP explanations deteriorates as class imbalance increases [36]. The finding changes the role of XAI in model evaluation. If explanations can be unstable, their quality cannot be judged by visual plausibility alone. Stability becomes an empirical property to be tested across resamples, periods, and model refits.

Other work on imbalance has concentrated mainly on predictive performance. Examples include a synthetic oversampling algorithm designed for imbalanced credit scoring [37], adaptive ensembles with imbalance mitigation for sector-level financial risk warning [11], and Bagging-XGBoost for highly imbalanced dynamic distress prediction [9]. Whether these data treatments also alter explanation stability is, as far as can be determined from the supplied material, not their central question. The link between data treatment and explanation stability is therefore directly evidenced in one study [36] and has not yet been examined systematically.

Imbalance also affects performance measurement. Accuracy is uninformative when events are rare. ROC-AUC is insensitive to class prevalence and says little about performance in the high-precision region relevant to alerting. Precision–recall measures and threshold-dependent quantities, such as the false-positive burden, are more informative for this purpose. None of these discrimination measures establishes that estimated probabilities are calibrated or that alerts arrive early enough to be acted upon. Sections 6 and 7 return to this issue.

## 5. Temporal risk and survival modelling
Credit risk is inherently temporal, yet a static classifier estimates an outcome over a fixed horizon without modelling when the event occurs or accounting for censoring. Survival analysis addresses both, and its use in credit scoring predates modern ML by some margin. Stepanova and Thomas applied survival methods, including extensions of the Cox proportional-hazards model, to personal-loan data [38]. Bellotti and Crook modelled time to default on credit-card accounts and incorporated macroeconomic variables as time-varying covariates [39]. These studies show that time-varying risk information is a long-standing concern in credit modelling and was not introduced by recent ML methods.

Recent work combines this tradition with ML and explainability. Xia et al. integrate survival analysis with gradient-boosted decision trees in a dynamic credit-scoring model and evaluate it in both out-of-sample and out-of-time settings [40]. Bone-Winkel and Reichenbach combine survival modelling with explainable ML in P2P lending [4]. JointLIME extends local interpretation to ML survival models with endogenous time-varying covariates [41]. Explanation of dynamic models is thus technically feasible. However, these studies do not by themselves show whether the explanations remain stable across time, or whether an earlier signal would be operationally useful.

Survival models also bring design obligations that fixed-horizon classifiers can avoid. The time origin, event definition, censoring mechanism, treatment of prepayment and account closure, handling of repeated loans, and the choice between borrower and exposure as the unit must all be specified. Survival modelling is therefore justified when the observed event process and portfolio history support it, not because it is inherently more advanced.

## 6. Early warning and latent deterioration before default
Early-warning research combines three distinct questions: whether an adverse event will occur within a given horizon, when it is likely to occur, and whether a preceding deterioration can be identified early enough to allow intervention. The literature addresses the first two far more extensively than the third. Financial-distress forewarning [8], dynamic distress prediction [9], industry-level risk monitoring for bank lending [12], sector-level financial risk warning [11], and early warning of severe profitability deterioration [14] all use horizons and interpretable ensembles. They do so, however, at the firm or sector level. Borrower-level studies mostly predict default itself [3], [4], [5], [6], [21], with survival models adding timing [40], [4], [41]. Some borrower-level work is framed explicitly as early warning; Wigantiyoko and Atok, for example, emphasise recall of defaults when selecting a model [21]. Such work nonetheless evaluates classification of the eventual event rather than the timeliness of the signal.

Studying latent deterioration requires a target that is defined independently of the model being evaluated. A deterioration label can be based on a pre-specified, observable transition, such as entry into a documented delinquency state, a downgrade in an external or pre-existing internal rating, or another portfolio-defined adverse transition. It should not be based on a rise in the candidate model’s own predicted probability of default, because that would make the target partly a function of the model trained to predict it. This requirement is a design principle of the present study and is not drawn from the cited literature.

Given such a target, early-warning performance involves more than correct classification of the final event. It depends on whether an alert precedes the event, on the distribution of lead times, on the share of events detected within an actionable horizon, on the rate of false alerts, on the persistence or reversibility of warnings, and on whether the explanation attached to each warning is stable enough to support review. The reviewed studies address several of these dimensions individually: horizons in the distress literature [8], [9], [14], event timing in survival models [40], [4], [41], and explanation stability under imbalance [36]. None of the supplied studies appears to evaluate them jointly at the borrower level; this observation is inferred from comparison across studies.

## 7. Calibration and decision usefulness
Discrimination and calibration answer different questions. A model may rank borrowers correctly while systematically over- or under-stating event frequencies. This matters whenever an output is used as a probability of default for segmentation, threshold setting, provisioning, or escalation. Rambauli et al. combine XAI with uncertainty quantification in credit-risk assessment and indicate that high predictive accuracy does not by itself guarantee reliable probability estimates [42]. Calibration curves, the Brier score, and calibration slope and intercept are the natural complements to ranking metrics when outputs are interpreted as probabilities.

Threshold selection raises a related issue. Maximising accuracy or F1 imposes an implicit trade-off between error types that may not match the costs of a monitoring process. Profit-sensitive classification in P2P lending [29] illustrates the broader principle that decision thresholds should reflect the economic consequences of errors. For early warning, the relevant costs include missed deterioration, the analyst workload created by false alerts, and the minimum lead time needed for an effective response. A model with a higher AUC may therefore be less useful for monitoring if it is poorly calibrated, produces excessive false alerts at the required sensitivity, or warns too late. Model selection accordingly becomes a multi-criteria problem, and the criteria and decision rule should be fixed in advance rather than combined after the results are observed.

## 8. Temporal validation, drift, governance, and auditability
When the intended use is ongoing portfolio monitoring, validation must respect time. Random train–test splits mix observations from different dates and can overstate performance when borrower behaviour, portfolio composition, macroeconomic conditions, or credit policy change. Out-of-time evaluation reduces this risk and is used explicitly in dynamic credit-scoring work [40]. Recent reviews of AI-based credit assessment identify concept drift, adaptive or real-time learning, and the practical operationalisation of explanations as unresolved challenges [43], [44].

A temporally sound design separates an earlier training window, a later validation window, and a final out-of-time test period. Every data-dependent preprocessing step, including feature selection, scaling, resampling, calibration, and hyperparameter tuning, should be fitted within the training window, and variables that encode future status must be excluded. The generalisation target should also be stated explicitly. If the same borrowers appear in earlier and later periods, the test evaluates future monitoring of an existing portfolio. Generalisation to previously unseen borrowers additionally requires a borrower-disjoint design. These recommendations are methodological positions of the present study and are not findings reported in the cited sources.

Governance adds external constraints. Bücker et al. treat transparency and auditability as model properties in credit scoring [2], and the model-risk literature extends validation to ML models used in banking [45]. The European Banking Authority’s Guidelines on Loan Origination and Monitoring require institutions that use automated models in creditworthiness assessment to address, among other things, data quality, traceability, auditability, robustness, and ongoing performance monitoring [46]. Recent frameworks likewise present explainability as a means of supporting regulatory compliance [47]. In practice, these requirements imply that a monitoring system should record model versions, data-snapshot dates, feature availability, thresholds, explanations, and subsequent outcomes. Governance is not the scientific contribution of the present study. Its role is to constrain the experimental design so that models and explanations can be reproduced and reviewed.

## 9. Synthesis, research gaps, and the present study
The reviewed literature forms five interconnected streams. The first is predictive modelling, in which ensemble and hybrid models often add flexibility but show advantages that depend on the dataset, target, class distribution, and validation design, while interpretable baselines remain competitive and useful [3], [5], [6], [20], [21], [1]. The second is temporal modelling, in which survival and dynamic approaches handle time to event, censoring, and time-varying covariates [40], [4], [41], [38], [39], but do not by themselves define an operational warning rule. The third is explainability, in which LIME, SHAP, and their extensions describe model behaviour locally and globally [24], [3], [27], [28], [25] and are increasingly tied to transparency, auditability, and fairness [34], [33], [2]. The fourth is evaluation under imbalance and change, in which imbalance is shown to affect explanation stability [36] and several methods address imbalance mainly as a predictive problem [37], [11], [9]. The fifth is governance and deployment, in which reviews, model-risk frameworks, and regulatory guidance extend evaluation toward monitoring, documentation, and control [43], [44], [45], [2], [46].

The gaps that follow from this literature fall into two categories.

### Limitations reported in the sources

Explanation stability degrades under class imbalance [36]. Recent reviews identify concept drift, adaptive learning, and the operational use of explanations as open problems [43], [44].

### Gaps inferred from comparison across studies

The following points are synthesis offered in this review and are not statements made by any single source. Early-warning studies with explicit horizons operate mainly at the firm, sector, or system level [8], [16], [11], [9], [12], [14], while borrower-level studies mostly predict eventual default [3], [5], [6], [21]. Temporal models demonstrate that time can be modelled and explained [40], [4], [41], but the stability of explanations across time periods is examined directly only in the context of imbalance [36]. Calibration, lead time, and false-alert burden are rarely reported alongside discrimination and explanation within the same study. The remaining gap is therefore not an absence of algorithms or of explanation methods. It concerns how these properties interact: a model that remains discriminative over time may still become poorly calibrated, warn too late, generate too many false alerts, or produce unstable explanations.

The present study addresses this interaction. At the borrower level and under an out-of-time design, it examines whether latent deterioration, defined through a pre-specified observable transition independent of the model, can be identified before formal default. It compares an interpretable baseline with more flexible models on the same information set and evaluates them jointly on discrimination, calibration, warning lead time, false-alert burden, and the temporal stability of their explanations.
