# Literature Review: Explainable Artificial Intelligence for the Early Identification of Credit Risk and Latent Deterioration in Borrower Quality

## 1. Theoretical and Methodological Foundations of Credit Scoring

Historically, credit scoring has been built around statistical ranking of the probability of default, with logistic regression retaining its value as a transparent baseline. The transition to machine learning has been driven by the ability of non-linear algorithms to capture feature interactions, borrower heterogeneity, and the complex structure of financial data. Breiman’s Random Forest method became one of the foundational ensemble approaches for later credit applications [1]. Its general methodological value, however, must not be conflated with evidence of effectiveness in a specific credit portfolio: source [1] is not an empirical credit-risk study.

In credit applications, the comparative effectiveness of ensembles depends on the problem formulation. Studies of P2P lending and scoring show that flexible models are used to assess risk with heterogeneous features and non-linear relationships [4], [6], [10]. In corporate and banking contexts, similar reasoning underlies models of default, financial distress, and industry-risk monitoring [9], [14], [29], [36]. The results cannot be transferred mechanically: P2P applications, listed companies, and bank portfolios differ in observed variables, data frequency, and the meaning of the target event.

## 2. From Ensemble Models to Applied Default Prediction

Contemporary studies commonly compare logistic regression with Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and hybrid architectures. In their study of interpretable default prediction, Yang et al. compare logistic regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM and use SHAP for interpretation; according to the abstract, ensemble methods perform better under non-linearity and imbalance [15]. Comparable applied settings are found in studies of multiple machine-learning models and SHAP interpretation [30], comparison of Random Forest and XGBoost for bank loan default [44], and a hybrid CNN–Transformer architecture for personal credit risk [35].

Despite improved predictive power, the model with the best ranking performance is not necessarily the most applicable in a credit decision. Methodologically, discriminatory power, probability calibration, and explainability of a rejection or alert must be distinguished. For this reason, the LightGBM-and-SHAP scorecard approach [37] is of interest as an attempt to bring an ensemble closer to a conventional credit scale, whereas ParamBoost [43] and general XAI frameworks [20], [28] are primarily methodological rather than credit-specific contributions. Thus, ensembles address the problem of flexibility but do not by themselves solve the problem of decision auditability. Bibliographic records indicate that study [45] concerns profit-sensitive machine-learning classification with explanations in P2P small-business lending; because a corresponding preserved analysis is unavailable, its specific empirical findings are not used in this review.

## 3. Interpretability as a Condition of Credit Applicability

The tension between accuracy and interpretability became central with the spread of black-box models. LIME established a general methodology for locally explaining an arbitrary classifier [2], and credit studies adapted this approach to explain P2P borrower scoring [4], risk management [5], [7], and credit-risk assessment [8]. Unlike an intrinsically transparent regression model, a post-hoc explanation describes a feature’s contribution to the decision of a particular model; it does not establish a causal effect. Bibliographic records indicate that the study by Liu, Zhang, and Xiong [25] concerns causal machine learning and a Bayesian-network approach to credit-risk prediction; because a corresponding preserved analysis is unavailable, its specific empirical findings are not used in this review.

SHAP is widely used in studies of default prediction, scorecards, network features, and fairness [15], [37], [42], [47], [49]. These studies share the aim of making ensemble decisions interpretable, but they differ in their level of analysis. Global feature importance is useful for portfolio management, whereas a local explanation is needed to justify a change in the assessment of an individual borrower. Studies of bias, transparency, and regulatory suitability [31], [41], [48] extend this line of work by linking explainability to fairness and governance. For several of these studies, however, only an abstract is available; their methodological findings should therefore not be extended beyond the reported information.

## 4. SHAP, LIME, Explanation Stability, and Imbalance

The critical development in XAI is not simply adding a SHAP visualisation to a model, but testing the stability of the explanation. Chen, Calabrese, and Martin-Barragan show that class imbalance affects the stability of SHAP and LIME interpretations in credit scoring [11]. An explanation may therefore change because of the training procedure, rather than only because of the economic structure of risk. This is especially important for rare default, where resampling, class weighting, and threshold selection jointly affect both metrics and interpretation.

Studies of oversampling [17], adaptive ensembles with imbalance mitigation [33], highly imbalanced dynamic-distress prediction [34], and SHAP-based feature selection [47] address this issue in different ways. Sources [17] and [33], however, are available only at the bibliographic-context level and cannot be assigned specific results. The substantively supported studies indicate that the rare-default problem requires evaluation not only of accuracy, but also of precision–recall, sensitivity, false alarms, and explanation stability. Incomplete reporting of these criteria remains a methodological limitation of the literature.

## 5. Dynamic Risk, Survival Analysis, and Early Warning

Xia et al.’s SurvXGBoost model [6] represents a fundamental departure from static default scoring. The authors combine survival analysis and gradient boosting for dynamic PD assessment, account for censoring, and compare models in out-of-sample and out-of-time settings. Unlike a classifier that answers whether default will occur over a fixed horizon, a survival model allows analysis of time to event. The development of this idea in P2P lending is reflected in the study of explainable survival analysis [10], although its evidence analysis is partial.

JointLIME extends the task through local explanation of black-box survival models with endogenous time-varying covariates [16]. This is a methodologically important step: temporal dynamics and explainability are considered jointly, but do not yet constitute a complete banking monitoring system. Studies of dynamic portfolio risk [21], dynamic-distress prediction [34], and industry-specific monitoring [36] confirm growing interest in temporal modelling, yet their objects differ. Corporate financial distress [9], [34], [50] and individual borrower delinquency are not equivalent; the resulting horizons and risk drivers cannot be treated as interchangeable.

## 6. Early Warning, Latent Deterioration, and the Boundaries of Default

Default prediction, financial-distress prediction, and early warning are related but not identical. Default is a formal event; distress may represent deterioration in financial condition before default; early warning requires that a signal have a useful lead time. Studies of financial-risk forewarning [9], severe profitability deterioration [50], systemic risk [12], [26], and supply-chain finance [19] broaden the understanding of an adverse outcome, but do not always model the trajectory of an individual borrower. For this reason, latent deterioration must be operationally defined before training, for example through a risk transition, a persistent change in PD, or a predefined delinquency state.

Comparison of these studies shows that high accuracy of a final classification is not equivalent to an ability to detect deterioration early. An applied early-warning solution must record the time of the first alert, the horizon to the observed event, and the cost of a false alert. Most reviewed sources analyse these elements separately. The issue is therefore not a lack of individual algorithms, but insufficient integration of problem-formulation requirements.

## 7. Calibration, Temporal Stability, and Model Governance

For credit risk, the probability of default must be calibrated as well as rank observations. ROC-AUC, F1, and accuracy do not replace a calibration curve, Brier score, PR-AUC, or recall at a fixed false-positive rate. Xia et al. [6] demonstrate the value of out-of-time testing, whereas literature on model-risk management and validation frameworks [40] places this issue within banking governance. Regulatory applicability is also associated with transparency, bias, and model audit [31], [41], [48], [49].

The evidence base is nevertheless heterogeneous: some publications are full empirical studies, while others are reviews or are available only as abstracts. The appropriate conclusion is not that calibration or monitoring is absent from all literature, but that their joint and comparable reporting is limited in this corpus. For a dissertation model, this means that temporal validation, PD calibration, drift monitoring, and a log of local explanations must be planned in advance.

## 8. Critical Synthesis

The relevant literature convincingly demonstrates the maturity of three streams: ensemble prediction of default and distress; XAI interpretation of credit decisions; and dynamic and survival approaches. The first provides predictive flexibility, the second provides a means of explanation, and the third provides the temporal structure of risk. Their integration, however, remains incomplete. Static SHAP solutions do not necessarily show how risk drivers change over time; dynamic survival models do not always test local explainability; and early-warning studies do not always assess calibrated PD or explanation stability under drift.

Comparison of empirical settings also requires attention to different target variables. In individual credit-scoring studies, default is generally treated as a borrower-level event [4], [6], [10], [15], whereas studies of listed companies analyse financial distress, profitability deterioration, or industry risk [9], [34], [36], [50]. Systemic risk [12], [26] belongs to a different level of aggregation. Thus, improved metrics in one segment are not direct evidence of applicability in another: feature availability, the economic cost of error, and the permissible warning horizon all change. For the present study, this means that the unit of analysis — the borrower — must be fixed and deterioration must be defined before an algorithm is selected.

The development of XAI also does not constitute a linear replacement of transparent models by complex ones. Logistic regression and scorecards are valuable because they provide a directly interpretable relation between features and an assessment, whereas ensembles require a separate explanatory layer. LIME [2] and SHAP approaches in credit applications [4], [11], [15], [37], [42], [47] answer different questions: a local surrogate explains an approximation of a decision in the neighbourhood of an observation, whereas SHAP allocates feature contributions to a model prediction. Their consistency is not guaranteed, especially with correlated features and changes in data composition. A local explanation should therefore be evaluated not only for plausibility, but also for repeatability across periods.

From an early-warning perspective, the distinction between a signal and an action is particularly important. A model may rank risk correctly but issue a warning too late or generate an unacceptable number of false alerts. Studies of forewarning and distress [9], [34], [50] show the importance of the predictive horizon, while dynamic and survival studies [6], [10], [16] demonstrate how time to event can be modelled. The available materials rarely report lead time, calibration, local explanations, and subsequent temporal testing together. The proposed design should therefore evaluate these properties jointly, rather than present them as consequences of a high ROC-AUC.

Practical applicability is further complemented by model governance. Studies of bias, fairness, validation, and regulatory compliance [31], [40], [41], [48], [49] indicate that an explanation must be linked to data-quality controls, validation rules, and the ability to audit a decision. At the same time, some of these publications are available only as abstracts, so their specific results are not extrapolated in this review. Their thematic focus nevertheless confirms that a banking implementation requires more than an accurate model: it needs feature documentation, temporal validation, drift monitoring, and a procedure for reviewing risk drivers.

## 9. Final Research Gap

Within the reviewed corpus, individual components of the problem have been substantively investigated. However, few studies simultaneously combine, at the individual-borrower level, early detection of latent deterioration before formal default, dynamic or survival modelling, calibrated PD, measurement of lead time and false alarms, temporal validation without leakage, local explanations, and tests of the temporal stability of those explanations within a governance and auditability framework. The dissertation design must therefore test their joint applicability, rather than infer it from one high metric.
